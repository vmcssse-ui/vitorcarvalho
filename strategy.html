§ 02Strategy and guidance report · Version 2.0 · October 2026

Adaptive by Design

An AI strategy for secondary schools that begins with inclusion: what the 2025–26 guidance asks of leaders, and a tested way to meet it. Written for headteachers, senior teams, governors and trust boards.

The argument

Start where the school will be judged

Download the report (PDF, 13 pages)

The guidance asks schools to judge AI by its effect on pupils, and to show that pupils who face barriers are reached. An AI strategy that starts anywhere other than inclusion will be measured against a question it was not built to answer.

Most AI strategies begin with tools: which assistant staff may use, which sites are blocked. This one begins with a classroom problem every teacher knows. A class of thirty contains pupils who arrive without the words the lesson will use, pupils who can do the task with one step shown, and pupils who need no help at all. Good teachers already adapt for all three. The difficulty is doing it every lesson, in every subject, without lowering what is expected of anyone.

AI makes that affordable. Writing a unit three times over at three levels of scaffold, with an audio script and a gate, is what makes adaptive teaching hard to sustain by hand. AI drafts it from the department's own lesson decks; a subject specialist checks and signs it. Decisions about individual pupils stay with people.

Summary of recommendations

Seven decisions for a senior team

The first three set the direction; the last four make it safe, sustainable and honest. Section references are to the full report.

  1. Write the AI strategy as part of the inclusion strategy, not beside it

    Ofsted grades inclusion as its own evaluation area and judges AI only by its impact on pupils. The 2026 white paper asks every school for an annual inclusion strategy. One document, one set of measures, one owner. [§03]

  2. Use AI where the evidence on adaptive teaching is strongest

    Pre-teaching, scaffolds that fade, and retrieval practice, with every pupil working towards the same task. This follows the EEF's guidance on SEND in mainstream schools and the SENSE study's warning that differentiation too often becomes lowered demand. [§03]

  3. Draw the boundary before the permissions

    Name where AI is not used: judging pupils, predicted grades, pupil reports, deciding whether a pupil has SEN, the content of an EHCP, emotional or mental-health support. The DfE's 2026 support materials draw the same line for SEND. [§05]

  4. Tier oversight by consequence, not by tool

    Specialist sign-off for anything asserting what an examiner rewards; subject-lead review for taught content; sampled checks for revision material derived from it. Name the tiers, the signatories and the record. [§05]

  5. Keep pupil data out by design, and put AI inside safeguarding

    Public pages hold no accounts and no pupil data. Any pupil-facing tool is checked against the DfE's generative AI product safety standards, and AI is in scope of the annual filtering and monitoring review that KCSIE 2026 requires. [§05]

  6. Build once, centrally, and put the time in a role

    Teachers using AI save time that is absorbed elsewhere, and four in ten have had no training. Start low on the risk slope and climb in order. Decide in advance where the time AI frees will go. Departments supply curriculum thinking; one team builds to one standard; staff are trained in the practice, not the tool. [§06]

  7. Agree the evaluation before the rollout

    Set the baseline first, and include measures that can return bad news, among them signs of cognitive offloading. Report what did not work to governors alongside what did. [§07]

The model

Four steps with one destination

It follows the EEF's guidance on SEND in mainstream schools, heeds the SENSE study's finding that differentiation too often becomes easier work, and puts retrieval before help, so that pupils do the thinking that cognitive offloading would take away.

1 Pre-teach

Before the lesson, the pupil hears and reads the words and prior knowledge it will rely on, in a read-along ebook where the text is highlighted as the audio plays.

2 Gate

A short check of two or three questions places the pupil at a starting level. Recall comes before any help is shown.

3 · Support

The steps and a worked example printed beside the task.

3 · Core

The steps hidden behind one hint.

3 · Stretch

No steps; the pupil explains and extends.

4 One destination

Every pupil finishes on the same question. As modules progress the scaffold fades, so support is a route in, never a lower ceiling.

The level changes how much help is visible, never the question.

Governance

The boundary, the tiers and the safeguards

Most material in an ecosystem like this is drafted with AI and edited by a subject specialist. A school needs a position on that, and a vague one will either block everything or permit everything.

The boundary

Where AI is not used

A policy that lists only what is permitted is not a position. Every item below matches either the DfE's 2026 support materials or data-protection law.

  • Making assessment judgements about pupils
  • Producing predicted grades
  • Writing reports on individual pupils
  • Deciding whether a pupil has SEN
  • Writing the content of an EHCP
  • Offering emotional or mental-health support

Oversight tiered by consequence

  • Tier 1 · highest consequenceAnything that tells pupils what an examiner rewardsSigned off by a second subject specialist
  • Tier 2Taught contentReviewed by the subject lead before pupils see it
  • Tier 3Revision material derived from reviewed contentSampled against its source

For each tier the school names who signs, and keeps a record that they did. A rule that every AI-assisted page must be checked line by line does not survive contact with two thousand lessons; a tiered rule does.

Implementation

Climb the risk slope

After Webster and Westerman's study of 21 large organisations for MIT Sloan Management Review: value came from smaller, deliberate changes ordered by risk, not one large transformation. In a school the customer is the pupil, so the order matters more. The DfE draws the same distinction between teacher-facing and pupil-facing uses.

Value and risk rise together. Climb in order

1 · Staff tasks

A school-approved tool and no pupil data: drafting, summarising, planning.

Lowest risk

2 · Curriculum material

AI drafts and a subject specialist signs: booklets, ebooks, modules, decks. Pupils meet checked material; they do not talk to an AI.

The Harlington work sits here

3 · Live pupil-facing AI

Tutors and chatbots: worth considering, but only after the product safety standards check, a data protection impact assessment and the KCSIE filtering review.

Highest risk

Why centrally

Build once, to one standard

A single department's success rarely spreads; a shared platform does. Departments supply the curriculum thinking and check the content; one team builds, so the workload sits in a role with the hours in it.

Saved time

Decide where it goes

Time freed by AI is not a saving until someone decides how it is spent. Name it in terms of pupils: pre-teaching groups, feedback on extended writing, the termly review of pupils on SEN Support.

Expertise

Second draft for a novice

Early-career teachers write their own adaptations first, then compare them with the AI draft under a mentor. AI is the second draft for a novice and the first draft for an expert.

In a new school

The first ninety days

Staff development follows the same order as the model: teachers practise pre-teaching, gating and fading scaffolds with material from their own subject. Generic whole-staff sessions on "using AI" are avoided.

  1. Map where pupils with SEND, disadvantaged pupils and pupils with EAL lose access to the curriculum, using the school's own data, before anything is built.
  2. Agree the boundary and the three tiers with the headteacher, the SENCo, the designated safeguarding lead and the data protection officer, and record them as policy.
  3. Add AI to the filtering and monitoring review, and check any pupil-facing tool against the product safety standards.
  4. Choose two departments and one unit each, starting with the teachers already experimenting with AI. Agree with them what success would look like by the summer.
  5. Take the baseline while one can still be taken.
  6. Build the two units on the four-step model, review them through the tiers, and teach with them.
  7. Report to governors at the end of the term on what worked and what did not.
Evaluation

Measures that can return bad news

Use is not impact; a page can be busy and useless. The measures are few, each is taken against a baseline, and two are designed to be able to embarrass the person proposing them. Only 35% of school leaders had any way of telling whether their technology was effective.

Honesty about the evidence

What the case claims, and what it does not

A strategy that only reports its successes cannot be trusted with its claims. An audit in August 2026 found some A-level pages carrying past-paper wording; a checking tool now runs before publication, and correcting those pages is the first action in any implementation plan.

Cost

No software to buy

Public pages are static files on free hosting; the authenticated layer is the Microsoft 365 licence a school already holds. The real cost is leadership time: someone senior who holds the strategy, runs the review workflow and leads the change in teaching.

Durability

Made to outlast one person

The school holds a deployable copy of its own pages; the terms on which the framework is used are written down; and the capability sits in a post with hours attached, not in one person's holidays.
